# The scene chart

The scene chart is what you see first when you open a play, and it's the heart of Play X-Ray. This page goes
through how to read it and what the three rows of buttons above it do.

![The scene chart of Hamlet, showing parts with 25 lines or more](images/chart-light.png)
*Hamlet, with the parts under 25 lines hidden.*

## Rows and columns

Each row is a speaking part, with its name on the left and its total number of lines on the far right, in the
same colour as its marks. Each column is a scene. The scenes sit under tabs for the acts, like the tabs in a prompt
book, and the little number under each tab is the scene ("2" under Act III is 3.2). Prologues and choruses are
marked "P" or "C", an Induction or an Epilogue gets its own tab, and red double rules run down the chart where
one act ends and the next begins.

The important thing is that every column is as WIDE as its scene is long. A 600-line scene takes up a lot of the
chart and a 30-line scene is a sliver, which means you see the rhythm of the play straight away: in Hamlet, 2.2
and 3.2 are huge, and Act IV opens with a run of short scenes one after another.

## The marks

A mark means that part speaks in that scene, and the stronger the colour, the more they say. The ten biggest parts
each get their own ink, which they keep everywhere in Play X-Ray, and the smaller parts are pencil grey.

Pencil hatching means the character is on stage but silent: a stage direction brings them on and they never say a
word. Look at the Ghost in Hamlet 1.1, who walks on twice and doesn't speak, or Lavinia in Titus Andronicus, who is
on stage in six scenes after 2.4 without a single line. That part is read from the entrances, so take it as a good
guide rather than a full record.

![A slip of paper over the chart saying Hamlet, 3.3: 24 lines, A room in the castle](images/chart-tooltip.png)
*Hover a mark and a slip of paper says who, where and how much.*

## Hovering, tapping and reading

On a computer, hover over any mark and a slip of paper tells you who, which scene and how many lines, for example
"Hamlet, 3.3: 24 lines", with the place the scene is set and how long it is. Click anywhere in a column and that
scene opens in the reader. On a phone or tablet, tap a mark to get the slip, then tap **Read 3.3** on it (or tap
the same spot again) to open the scene. The scene numbers along the top always go straight to the scene, and the
names on the left open that character in the [shared stage](cast-ribbons-stage.md#shared-stage).

## Order, Show and Width

**Order** puts the rows in order of **First entrance**, which reads like the play, or **Most lines**, which puts the
big parts at the top.

**Show** hides the small parts. **Everyone** shows every speaking part, including groups like "All" in grey italics,
and **10+ lines**, **25+ lines**, **50+ lines** and **100+ lines** keep only the parts that size and up. A play
like Henry VI, Part 2 has more than 60 speaking parts, so this is the quickest way to see the main story.

**Width** is **Fit**, which squeezes the whole play into the screen, or **Wide**, which gives every line of the play
a little more room and lets you scroll sideways inside the chart. On a phone, Fit is the overview and Wide is for
looking closely.

Play X-Ray remembers these three settings for next time, in your own browser.
